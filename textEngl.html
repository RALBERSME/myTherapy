Read the text out loud to me:



“If I understand you correctly, Mr. Schweigert: you want to overcome your inhibitions in order to spark up a conversation on a date? Correct?” Dr. Sockel looked over the rim of his glasses. On the elegant leather armchair opposite him, the diminutive man in the old-fashioned checkered blazer shifted uncomfortably.

“Not quite,” Kuno Schweigert countered promptly. “I am an excellent conversationalist. My goldfish can attest to that. On the first date, everything always goes perfectly. We meet at the Jazz Keller in Prinz-Eugen-Gasse—my regular haunt since my youth. A dark, vaulted cellar. The electric guitars vibrate so loudly through the masonry that you practically have to shout at each other.” A dreamy look stole into his eyes. “I talk about my plans. But mostly, I listen to the ladies. Every single one of these evenings ends with a hug. They whisper to me how fascinating I am, how attentive. That we must see each other again. Just like in the movies. We exchange numbers, and I can hardly wait for the next meeting.”

Dr. Sockel cleared his throat softly. “Promising indeed. And what brings you to me?”

“The second date, Doctor.” Schweigert’s voice dropped. Deep creases etched themselves into his forehead. “The second date is pure horror. As if a volcano erupted without warning.”

“What do you mean by that?”

“It starts out harmlessly. The lady moves closer, looks at me, and murmurs, ‘Tell me more about yourself.’ Only, there is nothing left. I’ve shot my entire bolt the first time around. But I’m too well-mannered to stay silent.” His fingers stroked the sheepskin on the armrest, then abruptly froze. “When I then repeat everything that moved them to tears the first time, word for word, exactly like that… that’s when disaster strikes.”

The therapist leaned forward. “Disaster?”

“They flee, Doctor! The women stare at me, grab their handbags, and run out of the place without a word.” He shoved his index finger between his teeth and gnawed on his nail.

“Strange,” Sockel murmured. “Not a single word of goodbye?”

“Nothing! Bag under the arm and gone.”

Dr. Sockel fixed his gaze on his patient. “Mr. Schweigert, we are going to roleplay this right now. I want to see what happens. I am the lady, and I’m sitting down at the table with you.” The therapist stood up, pulled over a chair, and sat down directly in front of Schweigert with a professionally inviting smile. “Mr. Schweigert, how wonderful to see you again. Last time I did so much of the talking. Why don’t you tell me a bit about yourself today? How do you picture our future?”

Schweigert flinched. He pressed his hand over his mouth. “Ahem, uhm…” The clearing of his throat escalated into a dry, violent cough. His cheeks turned crimson, his eyes watered.

Dr. Sockel broke character, poured a glass of tap water at the sink, and handed it to him. “Take a sip first.”

Schweigert nodded gratefully, wiped his watery eyes with the back of his hand, and drank hastily. He exhaled deeply.

“Good,” Sockel said. “Take a breath. Let’s start from the beginning. How do you picture our life together?”

Schweigert set the glass down so hard that the water sloshed over. “Our life together?” He stared at the doctor, bewildered.

“I am the lady from the jazz cellar,” Sockel reminded him patiently. “The second date. Please, Mr. Schweigert.”

The small man squared his shoulders, took a deep breath, looked Dr. Sockel dead in the eye, and said, “Blub, blub, blub…”

Sockel knitted his brow. His voice lost every ounce of therapeutic gentleness. “Mr. Schweigert, seriously now. What exactly did you tell the lady?”

“Blub, blub, blub,” burst out of Schweigert, stubborn and monotonous.

“If you’re trying to make a fool out of me, I’m leaving!” The therapist’s voice was cuttingly cold.

“You’re acting exactly like the ladies!” Schweigert hissed, jabbing his index finger in Sockel’s direction. For a moment, there was an icy silence. Then he slumped into his chair. “I just don’t understand it. I talk to my goldfish for hours. Animatingly.”

“So you blubber to yourself and wonder why no one listens to you.”

“But on the first date, the ladies liked it!” Schweigert’s mouth remained open, as if he were about to form the very next sound.

“In the jazz cellar, the roaring music presumably prevented your companion from catching any of this nonsense in the first place.” Dr. Sockel reached for his writing utensils.

“But my fish love it. They watch me through the glass. For hours.”

“Because they can’t escape their glass tank.” Sockel pulled out a black ballpoint pen. “If you don’t know how to talk to women, take this.” He handed him the pen. Schweigert leaned forward hungrily, his hand already outstretched toward the notepad on the therapist’s lap.

“Stop!” Sockel held the pad with an iron grip. “You don’t need paper. Just write one of the ten words I’m about to dictate to you onto each of your fingertips.”

Schweigert furrowed his brow.

“Don’t think. Write.” The therapist spoke the words like commands, his voice dropping an octave lower. “‘Tell’ goes on the thumb. ‘Understand’ on the index finger. Then ‘Feel, Why, And, How, Stop, Do, Safe, Thanks’ on the remaining digits. Got that?”

Schweigert obeyed hastily. Finally, he presented his blue-smudged fingertips to the doctor. “And what good is that supposed to do?”

“That is all you need to win a woman over. From the emergency kit of conversational therapy. For someone who otherwise only manages a ‘blub’, it’s more than enough.”

“And what exactly do I do with it?” Schweigert inspected the blue scribble on his skin skeptically.

“Just scatter these words randomly into the conversation. It will take care of itself from there. Consider yourself cured. The invoice will arrive by mail.” Dr. Sockel stood up and opened the door to the hallway.

Schweigert stared at him, at a loss. Then he looked at his index finger and read half-aloud, “Understand.”

“Excellent, how quickly you learn!” Sockel patted him fleetingly on the shoulder. “All the best.” With that, he left Schweigert standing in the corridor and closed the door.

The small man stared straight ahead. Only slowly did the experience sink in. Then his face brightened. An incredulous chuckle escaped him. “So it’s that simple,” he murmured. A deep sense of relief washed over him. He buried his face in his hands, pressing his palms against his forehead and pulling them down, as if stripping away a heavy mask.

When he lowered his arms again, his eyes seemed to shine with hope. He held his thumb at eye level to memorize the first vocabulary word to which he tied his entire future. He narrowed his eyes and thrust his chin forward.

On his skin, there was nothing but an indistinguishable blue smudge.

His heart skipped a beat. His gaze darted frantically over the rest of his hand. Everywhere, it was the same story. The edges of the letters had bled completely into one another — his skin had grown sweaty from the excitement. Dark, smeared streaks ran across his digits. Only here and there did a naked 'W' or the arch of an 'm' shimmer in the dim light of the hallway. Utterly stunned, he rubbed the ink with the heel of his hand, but it only made things worse.

A sudden wave of panic shot hot into his stomach. His knees turned to jelly, and a cold sweat broke out on his forehead. His hands were shaking so violently now that he could barely keep them still. He looked around wildly. The walls of the narrow corridor suddenly seemed to close in on him, choking the air from his lungs. Driven by a blind instinct to flee, he bolted down the hallway and into the men’s restroom.

“Maybe the prints on my face are still legible,” he whimpered, craning his neck. But the mirror above the sink hung far too high. Every hop and stretch was in vain. Scanning the room in a panic, Schweigert spotted the open stall door opposite him and, with a single leap, climbed onto the toilet seat. Carefully, he turned around.

He was at eye level now, but the sight was a nightmare. Dark blotches mottled his skin, connected by broad streaks that stretched from his forehead to his chin — the exact imprints of his sweaty hands.

Schweigert was so startled by his own reflection that his left foot lost its footing. He slipped off the seat and crashed deep into the toilet bowl. Water splashed high into the air. He flailed his arms wildly until, at the very last second, he managed to grab hold of the door frame. Panting, he pulled his trouser leg out of the drain. Water gushed from his expensive leather shoe, instantly forming a thick, dark puddle on the white tiles.

Kuno looked down at himself. Going back to Dr. Sockel to ask for a new list was out of the question. Not in this state. A biting cold was already creeping up his wet pant leg. As best he could, he wrung out the fabric over the tiled floor and hurried home.

At the kitchen table, the meticulous work began. Armed with a thick magnifying glass, he set to work on his own fingertips. For hours, he sat in the pale light of the kitchen lamp, transferring the blurry remnants of ink onto a sheet of paper. It was pure detective work, but in the end, he had his ten words assembled:

Elect
Hideout
Rummage
Warm
Dog
Never
Stubble
Clay
Seacher
Banks


Exhausted, yet buoyed by the feeling that this day would change his life, he crawled into bed and instantly fell asleep.

The next few days were entirely dedicated to learning. He copied the terms dozens of times. The sticky notes were everywhere: on the bathroom mirror, above the sink, next to the television. While brushing his teeth, peeling potatoes, and right before falling asleep, he drummed the dictation into his memory.

Friday evening. Kuno stood before the heavy wooden door of the "Golden Rooster." He dabbed the sweat from his forehead, fished the paper out of his jacket pocket one last time, scanned the lines, and stepped inside.

The restaurant was sparsely populated, the atmosphere subdued. The redhead with the plunging neckline didn't even have to wave. He recognized her instantly and headed straight for her table.

"Are you trying to hideout?" he called out to her from some distance away, throwing a blown kiss in her direction.

Marlies smiled and let her fingers glide through her pearl necklace. "My dear Kuno, you really are something. I already had a delightful time with you at the Jazz Keller. A true gentleman. Last time I practically talked your ear off. Why don't you start today: What do you dream of? And above all, how do you picture a life together?"

He pulled back the heavy oak chair and sat down. Marlies fished a bottle of sparkling wine out of the ice bucket and poured generously.

"Warm here, Marlies," Kuno said, clinking his glass against hers and draining it in one gulp. As he set it down, he looked deep into her eyes. Then his gaze slowly wandered down her red dress and lingered on her cleavage.

"What a fine clay," he murmured, fascinated.

Marlies closed her eyes for a moment in pure bliss, as if she had been waiting days for exactly this compliment. "It makes me so happy that you like my dress." She slid a little closer. "You wouldn't believe how hard it was to find this. I scoured countless shops. It was just before closing time when I spotted this masterpiece. And it's only for you." She leaned forward. "My ex-husband wouldn't even have noticed if I'd turned up naked for dinner."

Marlies had found her topic. For the next half hour, she mercilessly settled scores with her past: he had been selfish, unfaithful, and suspicious. Kuno sat beside her relaxed, sipping his wine and tossing in scraps like "that dog" or "elect" at just the right rhythm. She talked herself into a frenzy.

When a larger party entered the restaurant at a later hour and the background noise grew louder, Marlies moved very close to him. "Now I've gone and talked only about myself again," she murmured, looking longingly at his lips. "Now let me listen to you."

"A man with his first gray stubble," Kuno winked, shaking his head amusedly.

Marlies snorted with laughter and playfully nudged her elbow into his side. He gently placed his hand on hers. "The stubble!" she cried out in delight. "It reminds me so much of my late father. An absolutely wonderful man, and the stories he had up his sleeve..."

Another wave of anecdotes crashed over the table. Kuno smiled, nodded, and left the floor to her. The hours flew by. By the time the waiter was already clearing the empty glasses from the neighboring tables and extinguishing the candles, the departure was drawing near.

"We are going to meet again, aren't we, Kuno?" Marlies asked with glistening eyes.

"Never," he giggled, tenderly squeezing her hand.

"Never!" she squealed. "I've never received such a charming rejection in my life. So, when and where?" She fluttered her eyelashes wildly.

"At the banks." He winked at her, slipped into his jacket, and followed her outside.

Before the door, he pulled her close once more and whispered into her ear: "I am no seacher — I can still rummage!"

Marlies caught her breath. That sentence struck her right in the heart. It wasn't long before the wedding bells rang.




Kuno van Varnhagen was highly satisfied with Dr. Sockel’s performance. His life had been incomparably happier ever since. This was not only due to Marlies, whose surname he had willingly adopted at the wedding to wipe out his bleak past. His career was also on the rise. He had briefly tried his hand at mountain farming, but nature quickly lost its appeal. Why should he slave away with stubborn cows when a therapist could earn a fortune with a measly ten words a day?

A year later, the decision was made: Kuno opened his own practice. He considered a lengthy university degree entirely superfluous. The ten terms were rock-solid in his mind; you could have woken him at three in the morning and he would have recited them flawlessly.

Marlies once again proved to be an organizational genius. “If you grow a thick, full beard, you’ll look respectable right away,” she had dictated. “And as a therapist, you’ll sit on an extra-high chair behind the desk. People have respect for tall men.” She also helped him find a niche in the market and took over the secretarial duties from then on.

She placed advertisements, sent out info-emails, and mounted a shiny metal plate next to the front door:

Practice of Dr. van Varnhagen – Specialist in Marital Crises.

“Separations and divorces are dime a dozen,” she knew from her own experience. “It won't take long before the first emotional basketcase knocks on our door.”

She was right. Just a week later, she ushered the first patient into the room.

Kuno enthroned himself on his high chair, peering through the horn-rimmed glasses Marlies had procured for him. “They aren’t your prescription, but they make an impression,” she had whispered as she slipped the frame into the pocket of his white lab coat.

Kuno narrowed his eyes and tilted his head. Yet the figure in the room remained a blurry, wobbly outline. The glasses completely distorted his actually perfect vision. Never mind, he thought, pure matter of habit. With a vague wave of his hand, he pointed toward the large wingback armchair — an heirloom from Marlies’ first marriage.

“Take a seat.”

The dark silhouette set into motion. The leather of the chair groaned heavily under the weight.

“What brings you to me?” He had memorized this phrase well.

“My wife has moved out. Now she wants a divorce.” A deep groan emerged from the armchair. “I can’t bear the empty house. It’s pure hell.”

“In deed it`s warm,” Kuno rumbled deeply, stroking his expansive beard. Word number one.

“You know, I am a therapist myself. I run an excellent practice. Money and security are there. I anticipated my wife’s every wish. Travel, clothes, jewelry. She never complained. And suddenly she’s gone. What on earth did I do wrong?” The man fished a handkerchief from his vest and blew his nose loudly.

Kuno clung mentally to his list. Word number two was needed. “Does she have a searcher?”

The patient stopped dead in his tracks. “You hit the nail right on the head! Searcher, yes, that is exactly what you can call that old geezer. The guy she’s messing around with is easily ten years older than me. I hired a private detective to watch the two of them. He supplies me with photo and audio material. Not entirely legal, but I have to know what’s going on. She is my damn wife!” He struck the armrest angrily.

Kuno’s voice sounded hard and relentless: “Caught in flagrante at the banks.”

“Exactly! That old lecher. It’s perverse, throwing oneself at a woman in her mid-twenties when you’re a great-grandfather. Completely devoid of any morality.” The man blew his nose again. “And where and how they do it! Disgusting. Outrageous. I never subjected my wife to anything so filthy. Granted, I was already over… over fifty when we married. The herniated disc, you know how it is in our profession. From all the sitting around in the office. I’m sure it’s no different for you. So not much happens in bed anymore…”

The man worked himself into a frenzy, losing all restraint and describing the most intimate details of the detective’s report.

Behind his desk, Kuno felt the heat rush to his head. He felt sick to his stomach. His sweaty fingers slipped helplessly off the top button of his lab coat, which he was desperately trying to undo. He had to intervene. As the treating physician, he had to stop this flood of filth. He composed himself, but his brain felt completely wiped.

Through the lenses of the glasses, the room now blurred entirely into a psychedelic mush. The door frames seemed to melt, the ceiling light stretching into giant, floating rings. A violent dizziness seized him. Kuno clawed both hands into the edge of the desk to avoid tumbling from his high chair. Out of the fog, the patient’s voice boomed incessantly. The man noticed nothing of the naked panic currently paralyzing his counterpart.

Kuno groped blindly across the desk. His fingers searched for leverage. Finally, he shoved a finger under the rim of the glasses, pushed the frame forward a bit, and looked out underneath.

The world instantly snapped into sharp focus. Right in front of him lay his thick, black calendar. A white piece of paper peeked out from between the pages. His salvation.

Kuno took a deep breath, pulled out the list, and tapped blindly at a word to break the man’s torrent of speech:

“The clay.”

The patient fell silent mid-sentence. The nose-blowing stopped. The beefy figure in the chair slowly leaned forward. Kuno kept his finger on the paper.

Instant silence ensued. Kuno had let the horn-rimmed glasses slide back onto his nose. Through the lenses, he saw that the dark blotch in the chair remained still. He pushed up his coat sleeve: twenty minutes were up. Halftime, he thought. He dedicated himself to his note again, slid his finger down one line, and murmured after a brief clearing of his throat: “Without stubble.”

“Mr. van Varnhagen, I am sorry for troubling you with all this profanity,” a cracked voice drifted from the armchair. “You are absolutely right. These obscenities are not the heart of the matter. It eats me alive to have the perversity so crystal clear before my eyes. But it’s true: these are all just the stubble on the field. Not the roots.”

“No, never,” Kuno confirmed, crossing off the next word.

Another stricken silence. Kuno’s stomach gurgled audibly. It was fast approaching lunchtime — reason enough to end the session.

“Who is the dog?” he asked with renewed cheerfulness. Only three words left.

“The dog, my dear colleague… is of course myself. All these years I didn’t want to admit it to myself. Her beauty, her youth — that’s what attracted me. I had found out in our very first session that she goes for older men. It fit perfectly. Until this other geezer showed up…”

This was finally too much for Kuno. His stomach rebelled. From under the studio door, a treacherous waft of garlic crept in, spreading through the therapy room and making his mouth water. Marlies had taken the pizza out of the oven.

He had to cut things short. Kuno put everything on one card, took a deep breath, and squeezed the last three terms into a single, energetic sentence: “Stop trying to hideout! Elect! Rummage!”

Satisfied, he folded the list and smoothed out the creases with his thumbnail. At that very moment, a loud sobbing filled the room.

“That’s it! The root!” the man in the chair cried through his tears. “How blind I was! I have to get out of my den. Out of the practice, into the relationship. How foolish to sacrifice all this time for patients instead of for my sweet wife. Being there, listening, showing her that I see her. Addressing feelings, exchanging tenderness!”

Kuno observed a white blur wiping back and forth at the patient’s head level. Apparently, the man was drying his eyes. The goal had been achieved. Carefully, Kuno climbed down from his high chair. Because the glasses were blocking his path, he ripped the frame off his face as he walked.

He was only a single step away from the armchair when he froze dead in his tracks.

His vision was sharp again. And he knew the face of this man only too well.

Dr. Sockel! Kuno’s thoughts raced. How could this brilliant therapist have fallen so spectacularly from his own pedestal? He got no further, because Sockel jumped up, pulled Kuno into a warm embrace, and clapped him enthusiastically on the back.

“You are a master of your trade!” Sockel cried, full of admiration. “An absolute masterclass. Cutting straight to the point with the perfect interventions. You know your craft inside out. Congratulations! I failed to see the mote in my own eye, but thanks to you, my marriage is getting a second chance. I can feel it. More time, more attention, more love — that is what I will give to my wife.”

Sockel stepped back and looked at Kuno with shining eyes. “I won't be able to work as much, though. I need someone to take off the pressure. Tell me, why don’t we turn my practice into a joint venture? Then we can split the workload. How does that sound? Are you in?”

Kuno stared at him. This surpassed hitting the jackpot. Being handed half of an established practice on his very first day on the job bordered on a miracle.

A deep, grateful smile spread across his face. But what was he supposed to say? He looked helplessly over at the desk. Every single word on his list was crossed out. He had nothing left to express his solidarity. His eyes widened in panic.

But then, calmness returned to his gaze. Kuno placed his hand gently on the doctor’s forearm, looked him firmly in the eye, nodded with emotion, and said:

“Blub.”
